BASIC LAWS

6 ITEMS
1

LAW OF INTERPRETATION

Definition: A classification scheme must be interpreted according to its own structure, terminology, context and intention rather than by an arbitrary or purely literal interpretation.
Example: When a term occurs in a classification schedule, its meaning is determined in the context of the scheme and its relationship with neighbouring classes, rather than simply by ordinary dictionary meaning.
2

LAW OF IMPARTIALITY

Definition: Between two or more claimants, preference should be made only on sufficient grounds, and not arbitrarily.
Example: The sequence of facets in different subjects should not be decided arbitrarily in each subject. A general principle should be applied impartially to comparable cases.
3

LAW OF SYMMETRY

Definition: If two entities or situations are symmetrical counterparts and one receives weight or consideration in a contest, the other should receive corresponding weight or consideration unless there is a sufficient reason for treating them differently.
Example: If two corresponding classes or devices have equivalent roles in a classification system, they should receive corresponding treatment rather than giving one arbitrary preference.
4

LAW OF PARSIMONY

Definition: When two or more alternatives are available, preference should ordinarily be given to the one involving less expenditure of resources, effort or complexity, provided the essential purpose is adequately fulfilled.
Example: If two classification arrangements accomplish the same purpose, the one requiring less memory, less mental effort or less complicated operations is preferred.
5

LAW OF LOCAL VARIATION

Definition: A general rule may be varied locally when the conditions or requirements of a particular situation justify such variation.
Example: A classification principle normally applied generally may be modified in a particular subject or local situation when the special requirements of that situation make the variation desirable.
6

LAW OF OSMOSIS

Definition: Principles, practices and ideas developed in one part of library classification or library science may pass into and influence other parts when useful.
Example: A useful classificatory device developed for one area may gradually be adopted in other areas when it proves suitable there.
